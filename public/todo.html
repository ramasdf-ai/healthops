What you actually do each day and each week: the morning promise, the daily steadiness routine, and the weekly review. The background and reasoning stay in the plans linked above.

Morning promise

Every morning, make one promise to yourself and keep it. Trust in your own word only builds if the promises are sized so you actually keep them. One big promise you break teaches you that your word is cheap. Small promises you keep teach the opposite.

WOOP, old self to new self moderate

This version folds in Joe Dispenza's core idea from Breaking the Habit of Being Yourself: most of the day runs on autopilot, the same thoughts set off the same feelings and the same choices, and change starts when you catch the old self in the act and rehearse the new one ahead of time. That part maps cleanly onto WOOP. His bigger claims are sorted out further down.

Wish
Become someone whose word to myself is reliable.
Outcome
In a month, when I tell myself I'll do something, I believe it, the way I'd trust a friend who always shows up. Feel it for 30 seconds: the calm, the self‑respect. Then move on. Don't linger here.
Obstacle
Catch the old self. The old self promises big on good days, breaks it on bad days and quietly loses faith. Name today's version in three parts:
Thought: "I'll do it later, I'm wiped."
Feeling: flat, heavy, a little ashamed.
Behavior: phone, couch, skip.
Plan
If I notice "I'll do it later", then I do the minimum version right now.
If I can't keep the full promise, then I keep the minimum out loud, so it still counts.
Then rehearse it, eyes closed, 60 to 90 seconds. First person, in the real place: the thought arrives, you notice it, you name it ("that's the old self"), you stand up and do the first two minutes. Rehearse the steps, not the trophy.

Why the feeling step is capped at 30 seconds: Gabriele Oettingen, who built WOOP, found that vividly enjoying a goal as if it's already done can lower energy and effort. It helps only when it's followed straight away by the obstacle. Dispenza's "feel it as if it already happened" is the one step here most likely to backfire if done on its own, so it always comes before the obstacle, never instead of it.

What's borrowed from Dispenza, and what's left out

There's no proven way to rewrite the subconscious directly. What does work is pairing the same cue with a new response, day after day, until the new response becomes automatic. In the best‑known habit study that took a median of about 66 days, with a wide range. So expect around two months of daily reps, not a breakthrough in one session. moderate

Forcing the full hour onto a day that can't hold it is exactly the oversized promise this routine is meant to stop. On kid days, the short version counts as the whole routine.

Two rules that make it work

  1. One promise a day, not five. More promises mean more chances to break them, and breaking one sours the rest.
  2. The minimum always counts. A kept 5‑minute promise builds more trust than an abandoned 30‑minute one. Raise the minimum as the record gets longer. A good trigger is 14 kept days in a row. practical

The tally

The tally is the most important part. It lives in the "Today's promise" card at the top of the Rotation Daily Log. Mark each day as kept in full, kept the minimum, or not kept. The card keeps a running count, like "kept 26 of 30", plus your current streak. That gives you proof instead of a feeling about whether you're reliable.

Daily steadiness routine

This is self‑care, not treatment. It works whether or not any diagnosis comes back, and it gives the clinician better data. It uses the journaling, meditation and stress tracking already in the strength plan, plus a few skills borrowed from DBT, a therapy built for strong and fast‑moving emotions. Learning skills on your own helps, though a skills group or therapist adds more. moderate

Four skills, one per week, then repeat

Stop
When a reaction spikes: stop, take a step back, observe what's happening in your body and the room, then proceed on purpose. Practice it on small irritations first, so it's there for the big ones.
Cool down
For intense moments: cold water on the face for 30 seconds, a minute of hard movement such as squats or a fast walk, then slow breathing. It shifts your body state quickly, which is why it's in DBT's crisis set. moderate
Check facts
Write the event, the story you're telling about it, and the emotion's intensity from 0 to 10. Ask whether the intensity fits the facts. Use it in the evening journal.
Opposite action
When an emotion doesn't fit the facts, act opposite to its urge. Withdrawing when low? Text one person. Want to snap? Lower your voice and slow down.

DBT's basic self‑care set (sleep, food, exercise, avoid mood‑altering substances) is already covered by the strength plan. That overlap is deliberate.

Across the rotation

Free days

  • Full ten minutes, with a 10‑minute meditation.
  • Weekly review on the first free evening: read the week's journal lines, note the skill that helped, pick next week's skill.
  • One in‑person social plan per free block.
  • Evaluation prep tasks, such as calls, forms and the ASRS, go here.

Kid days

  • Minimum version: mood score, one journal line, 3 minutes of breathing after bedtime.
  • Use Stop and Cool down in the moment. Kids notice a parent who pauses.
  • Outdoor time together counts toward movement and mood.

Handoff days

The first evening alone is often the lowest point in the loop. Plan it ahead: a training session, a call with a friend, or dinner out, then the evening routine. Log a mood score that night even if you skip everything else, because that number will show whether handoffs drive the dips. practical
